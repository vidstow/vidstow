Signed-in downloads with browser cookies

Here's the idea: instead of typing your Google password into VidStow, the app simply borrows the YouTube login you already have in Safari or Chrome. If you never touch anything that needs a sign-in, you'll never notice a thing — and your cookies are never saved, never shown, and only travel alongside that one video's requests.

0 · VidStow in 30 seconds

If you're new here, welcome. VidStow is a small desktop app that saves public YouTube videos onto your own machine — no account, no cloud, nothing phoning home. The whole app is just four places: Home takes links in, Queue shows what's downloading right now, Downloads keeps your finished files, and Settings holds your defaults.

Every download works the same way. You paste a link on Home and hit Analyze, and the app looks the video up for you. Then a little panel called the dock offers readable choices — things like 1080p, subtitles as a sidecar file, and title details written into the file itself. Hit Download and it joins the Queue; when it's done you'll find it in Downloads, where Open plays it and Reveal shows it in your file manager.

1 · The walkthrough: one link end to end

Picture this: you paste a members-only teaser that your account is allowed to watch. Analyze tries anonymously first, the way it always has. When YouTube answers "sign-in, please" and you've already picked a browser, VidStow spends your session on one automatic retry before you've lifted a finger — and the dock just appears. No card, no extra click. You only ever meet the error card when there's nothing configured, or when your session genuinely couldn't open the video.

You sign in exactly where you always sign in. Open Safari or Chrome, sign in to YouTube there like normal, then come back to VidStow's Settings and pick that browser from a single dropdown. There's deliberately no test button — the next signed-in lookup is the test. On a Mac, Chrome might pop up its usual Keychain prompt at that moment — that's expected, just approve it.

From there, it's the VidStow you already know. The dock shows up with the same Output and Subtitles rows, Download queues the file, Queue shows it moving live, and Downloads keeps the finished row. A small session line under the link field notes it ran signed in via Chrome, just for that check — and the status bar at the bottom keeps showing only your folder and speed, exactly like today. And when you already know a link needs it, the flip line under the field spends the session up front, skipping the failed round-trip entirely.

2 · How 4K handles it

4K does it all inside the app. There's a Log In button in its control panel that pops open a Google window — one Google often mistakes for an iPad, complete with the occasional CAPTCHA or second-factor check. Once you're in, 4K can grab age-restricted clips plus your Liked videos, Watch Later, Premium stuff, and any private playlists your account can already watch.

Handy, but it comes with baggage. Private downloads are a paid 4K feature, your session lives inside 4K itself, and suddenly logout handling and background behavior are all 4K's problems. VidStow can get most of that value without taking on any of it, just by reading the browser you already trust.

4 · How the pieces connect

Nothing lingers — that's the whole point. A single-video lookup, a playlist lookup, the per-link checks inside a batch, and the download run itself each get the same two engine values, good for that one attempt only. And if a stale plan forces Download to re-run its lookup first, that refresh quietly reuses the same choice rather than flipping you back to signed-out mid-flight. Settings remembers just the browser's name and the cookie file's path — neither of which is a secret — while the cookies themselves live and die inside that single engine call.

Retry plays fair, too. With the recommended Retry answer in place, each Queue row writes down which browser choice its attempt used, so retrying with sign-in repeats the same session instead of silently going public on you. Leave Settings on Off and every check stays public until you say otherwise — and the status bar down below keeps showing nothing but your folder and your speed.

Contracts — everything a build needs

Below is the part of the proposal an implementer works from directly: final names, wire formats, and who calls whom. Anything still waiting on section 10 wears its question tag openly — needs Q1 and friends — so nothing looks decided that isn't.

C1 · The two Settings fields

Two plain strings join the Settings document, validated and persisted exactly like the download folder. The names below are final, not illustrative.

FieldJSON keyEmpty meansRules
BrowserSessionbrowserSessionSigned outShape browser[:profile][::container], passed straight to the engine's CookiesFromBrowser. The dropdown constrains the browser name; free-typed profiles mirror the engine's own rules — no empty, dot-only, or :/\ characters, containers only with Firefox.
CookieFilecookieFileNo fallback filePath text only, same length rules as the folder. Existence is deliberately not required at save time — the file might live on a disk that isn't plugged in, and the engine reports that at use time. Needs Q1: hidden entirely if the answer is browser-only.

No migration needed: absent keys decode to empty, which already means signed out, and older builds ignore keys they don't know. Writes travel the usual settings path under the settings lock.

C2 · The keyed error between Go and the UI

Here's a wrinkle worth knowing: Wails hands the frontend nothing but an error string, so "this failed for sign-in reasons" needs a machine-readable shape smuggled inside it. A new error type carries a stable JSON envelope — reason, browser, message — and a small parser reads it back. Anything malformed falls through to today's generic fallback, so old and unexpected errors behave exactly as now.

C3 · How the choice reaches the engine

The Manager grows a setter that mirrors the FFmpeg one line for line: mutex-held, future attempts only, in-flight work keeps its snapshot. The app calls it once at startup and again on every settings save. And here's the rule that makes the whole feature feel invisible — every Analyze first tries exactly as today, signed out. Only if the engine comes back with an authentication failure, a session is configured, and this attempt hasn't spent one yet, does it go again with the snapshot attached. Exactly once, same URL, sign-in failures only: never timeouts, never rate limits, never challenge pages. The frontend learns about the second attempt through a tiny progress event so the waiting card can honestly say it's trying the browser session.

Beside that automatic path sits the express lane from Diff E. Flipping the line passes an explicit with-session flag on single-video Analyze — one attempt with the snapshot up front, no anonymous round first, and a signed-in failure there yields the session-failed card directly since there's nothing weaker to fall back to. The mode lives only in the window session and resets to public the moment the link changes, the same trigger that clears analysis. Playlists and batches keep the automatic rule with no upfront toggle in v1 — bulk flows shouldn't strobe prompts, and the failed round-trip costs them proportionally less.

Downloads need no new request fields: starting one simply attaches whatever Settings currently holds, which is harmless on public videos and required on private ones. Playlist lookups, batch review-agains, and the stale-plan refresh all follow the same try-anonymous-first rule — per child where children are resolved one by one. One caveat to file away: on macOS each of those attempts can raise its own Keychain prompt until the user approves with Always Allow. Needs Q3: the manual mode, if it wins, keeps an explicit per-check button instead of the automatic retry.

C4 · Queue specifics

Two failure flavors, deliberately different faces. A video that needs sign-in says so and offers Retry plus the new Open Settings button. A session the app couldn't even read says that instead — the failure is on this Mac, not on YouTube — and skips the Open source and Copy link buttons, since the page is probably fine and they'd only add noise. The lead button follows suit in both the inspector and the little recovery button beside the row, and the evidence details stay exactly as they are.

C5 · Why there's no Check button in v1

This one stung a little: the cookie readers live in the engine's internal packages with no public "just verify, don't download" call, so a Check button cannot be built against the public API — anything pretending otherwise would be theater. Version one drops it; the Status line echoes your pick and the next signed-in lookup is the real test. If the engine ever exports a dry-run import, Check comes back as a one-row addition.

C6 · PRODUCT wording, ready to paste needs Q2

Supported gains one line: bounded signed-in YouTube downloads through your own browser session — age-restricted clips and private items your account can already watch. The out-of-scope list loses authenticated downloads and trades its cookies-and-login line for password vaulting, in-app login screens, and access-control circumvention. And the principles gain a sibling for the diagnostics rule: borrowed sessions, never stored — VidStow reads your browser's session per check and keeps nothing but the browser's name. One honest footnote: that scope is enforced by promise and copy, not by pre-filtering, because nothing can reliably tell an age gate from a private video before trying.

C7 · How it gets tested

Settings validation gets table-driven cases including traversal-shaped profiles; the keyed errors get round-trip tests plus malformed-input fallbacks; the classifier gets Op-based cases built from engine error values, never text; Queue gets copy and capability matrices for both failure flavors; the frontend gets its type check plus the helper's unit tests. Then the human matrix: each desktop OS against Chrome, Firefox, and Safari or Edge, each in signed-out, healthy, missing-profile, denied-prompt, and bad-file states.

9 · Risks kept visible

Heads up on shared machines: history shows titles. A private video that downloads just fine will still sit in Downloads and Queue under its private title — worth knowing if anyone else sees your screen. The row keeps its collection label, but never, ever its cookies.

And treat a cookie file like a house key. It holds sessions for every site, not just YouTube, so the picker warns you before accepting one — and clearing the row forgets the path entirely. Diagnostics, Copy diagnostics, and the failure evidence stay redacted exactly as today, gaining no new fields whatsoever.

Signing in buys you no immunity from rate limits. It won't excuse a 43-video admit or a concurrency past 4, and the familiar slow-down wording stays word for word.
